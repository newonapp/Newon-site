SHAREON

SHAREON Business — From an ownership-centred market
to a usage-centred economy.

Built on physical-asset sharing and rental between people and businesses, SHAREON aims to be a platform that expands into more asset categories, transaction support, business solutions and a global sharing-economy ecosystem. From the 20 areas first considered, the six furthest from sharing and rental were set aside, leaving 14 core businesses.

BUSINESS STRUCTURE

Starting with C2C,
then B2C, C2B and B2B.

External trades fall into four structures, and moving assets inside a company is handled separately. Not all of them are operating.

Borrower →PersonBusiness

Lender ↓PersonBusiness

  • C2C

    Person → person

    A neighbour borrows something another neighbour owns. This is the trade SHAREON focuses on first, centred on small items such as camping gear, tools and baby gear.

  • C2B

    Person → business

    A business borrows an individual’s equipment, such as a camera kit, for work or an event. This is a rental with an individual as supplier, not a wanted or request board.

  • B2C

    Business → person

    Small businesses and companies with equipment or spaces, such as camera rental shops or space operators, lend to individuals. Opens after business verification.

  • B2B

    Business → business

    A business lends assets such as event or filming equipment to another business. Bulk and long-term rentals are reviewed with separate contract terms.

  • 05

    Internal B2B

    Internal asset sharing

    Moving assets between employees and departments is kept apart from external paid trades. A SaaS subscription fits this better than a per-trade fee.

  • 06

    B2B2C · B2G

    Partners and institutions — under review

    Structures where a rental shop and a delivery or insurance company serve individuals together, and shared equipment at universities or institutions, are possibilities for discussion and not confirmed business areas.

SMALL-TICKET ECONOMICS

The smaller the rental,
the less is left.

Small-item rentals in a neighbourhood are low in value per trade. This profitability problem is handled openly at the design stage.

  1. 01

    Deposits are not revenue

    A deposit is handled separately through the payment provider and must be returned. It is left out of fee calculations and never deducted without legal or contractual grounds.

  2. 02

    Every trade carries costs

    Card processing fees, payout costs and VAT apply to every trade, however small. Internal calculations show that a rental of a few thousand won can lose money even after the fee.

  3. 03

    So a cost floor comes first

    Minimum fees, a rate floor and costs by payment method are under review, and trade structures where the fee falls below payment, refund, fraud and support costs are not to be allowed. Transaction value, revenue and contribution margin are tracked as different numbers.

MARKET & DIFFERENTIATION

The market problem
and our hypotheses.

These are hypotheses about the opportunity and competition. Figures such as market size or share are not verified and are not shown.

  1. 01

    Opportunity — the cost of buying rarely used things

    Buying and storing things you rarely use costs money and space. The starting problem is that there are not enough ways to borrow for just the period needed within a neighbourhood.

  2. 02

    Gap — today’s alternatives

    With community posts and friends, schedules, security and return checks are left to the people involved; high-street rental shops are limited in location and stock; second-hand selling services are built around transferring ownership. There are also rental services specialised in one field, such as fashion.

  3. 03

    Hypothesis — a flow built for rental

    If booking calendars, double-booking prevention, pick-up and return records, deposit terms and disputes are designed for rental from the start, strangers in a neighbourhood will find it easier to lend and return.

  4. 04

    Hypothesis — one platform for people, businesses and internal use

    Handling personal rentals, business rentals and internal sharing on the same engine should grow supply and let operating tools be reused.

  5. 05

    Risk — barriers and regulation

    Building early supply and demand in a two-sided market, the cost of handling disputes, and e-finance and marketplace-intermediary rules and terms for payments and deposits are the biggest challenges.

OPERATING & LEGAL PRINCIPLES

Operating and legal
principles.

What must be checked area by area before the business grows. Legal review is planned and no opinion has been received yet.

  • Trades between people set out contracts, responsibilities and consumer protection clearly in the terms.

  • Business listings follow business verification and e-commerce and marketplace-intermediary duties.

  • Payment and settlement open only after the payment-provider contract and e-finance requirements are confirmed. NEWON does not hold funds.

  • Deposits have defined holding, return and deduction grounds and are never counted as revenue.

  • Damage and loss are handled through compensation rules, evidence and a dispute process.

  • Space rental checks permitted use, permits and facility rules first; insurance links check insurance-selling rules first.

  • Business assets respect ownership and staff access rights; personal data such as contacts, addresses and trade details is kept to a minimum.

  • Tax duties are reviewed for each party to a trade, and overseas expansion follows each country’s rental and payment rules.
